\chapter{Methodology}
\label{ch:method}

This chapter operationalises the research questions developed in
\Cref{ch:intro} through a controlled experimental framework for evaluating
whether diffusion-based refinement of textual conditioning improves
compositional generation in text-to-image (T2I) diffusion models. Building on
the conditioning-distortion framing introduced in the preceding chapters, the
methodology compares raw prompts against prompts refined by two distinct
language models, one autoregressive and one masked-diffusion, under an
otherwise identical generation pipeline. The framework extends the original
proposal in three respects. First, the refinement operator, previously left
abstract, is instantiated as a masked diffusion language model whose iterative
unmasking process provides a principled mechanism for semantic refinement.
Second, encoder capacity is treated as an explicit experimental axis by
substituting the T2I backbone rather than the encoder in isolation. Third, a
language-side Product-of-Experts operator is introduced as a compositional
mechanism that, as argued below, is available only to the diffusion model. In
this way, the methodology isolates two distinct effects: the contribution of
refinement quality, and the contribution of the refinement mechanism itself.

\section{Overview of the Conditioning Pipeline}
\label{sec:pipeline}

The experimental design compares four conditioning configurations that differ
only in how the conditioning text is produced. In each case the resulting text
is encoded by an identical, frozen T2I backbone, so that any observed
difference in generated images may be attributed to the conditioning text
rather than to the image generation model \citep{rombach2022ldm}. Given a raw
prompt \(t\), the four configurations are defined as follows:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Raw conditioning:} the prompt is used directly,
        \(c_{\mathrm{raw}} = f_{\mathrm{enc}}(t)\).
  \item \textbf{Autoregressive refinement:} the prompt is expanded in a single
        left-to-right pass by an instruction-tuned autoregressive model,
        \(c_{\mathrm{ar}} = f_{\mathrm{enc}}(g_{\mathrm{AR}}(t))\).
  \item \textbf{Masked-diffusion refinement:} the prompt is expanded by a
        masked diffusion language model through iterative unmasking,
        \(c_{\mathrm{llada}} = f_{\mathrm{enc}}(g_{\mathrm{LLaDA}}(t))\).
  \item \textbf{Product-of-Experts composition:} the prompt is decomposed into
        \(N\) atomic constraints that are composed jointly by the diffusion
        model into a single refined description,
        \(c_{\mathrm{poe}} = f_{\mathrm{enc}}(g_{\mathrm{PoE}}(t))\)
        (\Cref{sec:poe}).
\end{itemize}

where:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \(t\) denotes the raw user prompt,
  \item \(f_{\mathrm{enc}}(\cdot)\) denotes the frozen text encoder of the T2I
        backbone,
  \item \(g_{\mathrm{AR}}, g_{\mathrm{LLaDA}}, g_{\mathrm{PoE}}\) denote the
        respective refinement operators.
\end{itemize}

The autoregressive and masked-diffusion branches receive an identical expansion
instruction, so that any difference between them may be attributed to the
generative mechanism rather than to the wording of the instruction. This design
choice is central to the study: because the two refinement models are prompted
identically and evaluated under matched conditions, the comparison isolates the
effect of the underlying generative process. The single-pass masked-diffusion
branch and the Product-of-Experts branch share the same model and decoding
hyperparameters, and differ only in whether the constraints are expanded
jointly within one prefix or composed multiplicatively as a product of experts.
From this perspective, the contrast between these two branches isolates the
composition operator itself from the diffusion mechanism on which it is built.

\section{Masked-Diffusion Refinement and Product-of-Experts}
\label{sec:llada-math}

This section formalises the two generative procedures used to produce refined
conditioning text. The first is standard masked-diffusion generation, in which
a single prompt is expanded through iterative unmasking. The second is the
Product-of-Experts operator, in which multiple constraint-conditioned
distributions are combined multiplicatively over a shared response.

\subsection{Masked Diffusion Language Models}

Masked diffusion language models extend the diffusion paradigm to discrete
token spaces, treating text generation as the progressive recovery of a
sequence from a fully corrupted state \citep{austin2021structured,
li2022diffusionlm}. Let \(V\) denote the vocabulary and let \(\mathbf{M} \in
V\) denote a distinguished \textbf{mask} token. A response is a sequence
\(\mathbf{r} = (r_1, \dots, r_L)\) of fixed length \(L\), generated conditioned
on a prompt prefix \(\mathbf{c} = (c_1, \dots, c_P)\). The model operates over
the concatenated canvas:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x} = (\underbrace{c_1,\dots,c_P}_{\text{prefix}},\;
             \underbrace{x_{P+1},\dots,x_{P+L}}_{\text{response}})
\end{equation}

where:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \(\mathbf{c}\) is the fixed conditioning prefix of length \(P\),
  \item the response region of length \(L\) is initialised as fully masked and
        is progressively resolved,
  \item \(\mathbf{M}\) is the absorbing mask state.
\end{itemize}

Whereas an autoregressive model factorises the joint distribution as
\(p_\theta(\mathbf{r}\mid\mathbf{c}) = \prod_{j} p_\theta(r_j \mid r_{<j},
\mathbf{c})\) and commits each token before the next is considered, a masked
diffusion model defines \(p_\theta(\mathbf{r}\mid\mathbf{c})\) implicitly
through an iterative unmasking process that resolves the entire response
jointly. The forward corruption process replaces each response token
independently with \(\mathbf{M}\) according to a schedule, forming an
absorbing-state diffusion in which a masked token remains masked
\citep{austin2021structured}. The model \(f_\theta\) is trained to invert this
process, predicting the clean token at each masked position given the partially
masked canvas and producing per-position logits \(\boldsymbol{\ell} =
f_\theta(\mathbf{x})\). From this perspective, generation may be interpreted as
the progressive recovery of latent structure from a corrupted observation,
mirroring the denoising formulation of continuous diffusion in discrete space
\citep{li2022diffusionlm, austin2021structured}.

\subsection{Generation by Iterative Unmasking}

Generation reverses the corruption process. Beginning from a fully masked
response, tokens are progressively committed in order of model confidence. To
balance parallelism against sequential coherence, the response of length \(L\)
is partitioned into \(B\) contiguous blocks of size \(L_b\), with \(B = L /
L_b\), which are resolved from left to right. Within a block the process is
parallel, in the manner of diffusion, while across blocks it proceeds
sequentially; setting \(L_b = L\) recovers fully parallel generation. The total
step budget \(S\) is divided evenly across blocks, giving \(S_b = S/B\) steps
per block.

At each unmasking step, the model first evaluates the current canvas and reads
off, for every position, the most probable token together with its confidence:

\begin{equation}
\hat{x}_j = \arg\max_{v \in V} \tilde{\ell}_{j,v},
\qquad
\pi_j = \big[\mathrm{softmax}(\boldsymbol{\ell}_j)\big]_{\hat{x}_j}
\end{equation}

where:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \(\hat{x}_j\) is the greedy prediction at position \(j\),
  \item \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\) denotes the logits after optional
        temperature perturbation (\Cref{eq:gumbel}),
  \item \(\pi_j\) is the predicted-token confidence under the low-confidence
        remasking criterion.
\end{itemize}

Confidence is then restricted to the current block and to positions that remain
masked, and the \(k_s\) highest-confidence positions are committed to their
predicted tokens. The number of tokens committed at each step follows a
schedule that distributes the \(m\) block-initial masks as evenly as possible
across the \(S_b\) steps:

\begin{equation}
k_s = \left\lfloor \frac{m}{S_b} \right\rfloor
      + \mathbb{1}\!\left[s < (m \bmod S_b)\right],
\qquad \sum_{s=1}^{S_b} k_s = m
\end{equation}

so that exactly \(m\) tokens are unmasked over the course of the block. This
may be interpreted as a progressive commitment schedule in which the model
resolves the most confident predictions first, deferring uncertain positions to
later steps once more of the surrounding context has been fixed.

Sampling stochasticity is controlled through a Gumbel-max reparameterisation. For
temperature \(\tau = 0\) the logits are used unchanged, yielding deterministic
greedy decoding, while for \(\tau > 0\):

\begin{equation}
\tilde{\ell}_{j,v}
= \frac{\exp(\ell_{j,v})}{(-\log u_{j,v})^{\tau}},
\qquad u_{j,v}\sim U(0,1)
\label{eq:gumbel}
\end{equation}

which yields a temperature-scaled categorical sample. Throughout this study
\(\tau = 0\) is used, so that refinement is a deterministic function of the
prompt. This determinism permits refined prompts to be cached by prompt string,
an important practical consideration given the computational cost of masked
diffusion inference.

\subsection{Product-of-Experts Composition}
\label{sec:poe}

Product-of-Experts (PoE) provides a compositional probabilistic framework in
which several conditional distributions are combined multiplicatively to form a
joint distribution \citep{hinton2002training, du2019implicit,
liu2022compositional}. A compositional prompt bundles several constraints, for
example a colour bound to one object, a colour bound to another, and a spatial
relation between them. Single-pass refinement must encode all such constraints
within one conditioning prefix, whereas PoE instead assigns one \textbf{expert}
to each constraint and requires the experts to agree on a single shared
response.

Each constraint \(i \in \{1,\dots,N\}\) is templated and tokenised into its own
conditioning prefix \(\mathbf{c}^{(i)}\), and each expert conditions the same
shared response region \(\mathbf{r}\). At every unmasking step, each expert
scores the shared response independently, yielding per-position
log-distributions \(\log p_i(v \mid j)\). The experts are then combined by
summing their log-probabilities, which is equivalent to multiplying their
distributions, and renormalising:

\begin{equation}
p_{\mathrm{PoE}}(v \mid j)
\;\propto\;
\prod_{i=1}^{N} p_i(v \mid j)
\;=\;
\exp\!\Big(\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{N} \log p_i(v \mid j)\Big)
\label{eq:poe}
\end{equation}

where:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \(N\) is the number of constraint experts,
  \item \(p_i(v \mid j)\) is expert \(i\)'s distribution over vocabulary token
        \(v\) at response position \(j\),
  \item \(p_{\mathrm{PoE}}\) is the renormalised product distribution.
\end{itemize}

Under this formulation a token receives high combined probability at a position
only if every expert independently assigns it high probability; where the
experts disagree, the product is small. Consequently the composition selects
tokens that are jointly consistent with all constraints, in contrast to
concatenating the constraints into a single prompt and relying on one
conditional distribution to balance them. Unmasking proceeds as in the standard
case, but is driven by \(p_{\mathrm{PoE}}\) and applied identically to every
expert so that the shared response remains synchronised. This procedure may be
interpreted as targeting the classical Product-of-Experts distribution
\citep{hinton2002training}:

\begin{equation}
p_{\mathrm{PoE}}(\mathbf{r}\mid \mathbf{c}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{c}^{(N)})
\;\propto\;
\prod_{i=1}^{N} p_\theta\big(\mathbf{r}\mid \mathbf{c}^{(i)}\big)
\end{equation}

approximated by per-position products at each unmasking step. This
per-position approximation is a mean-field simplification of the full joint
product, a point returned to in the discussion of limitations.

A practical requirement of this construction concerns positional alignment.
Because the model relies on positional encodings, the shared response tokens
must occupy the same absolute sequence positions in every expert's canvas.
Where this condition does not hold, each expert scores the response under a
different positional context, and the summed log-probabilities in
\Cref{eq:poe} collapse onto position-invariant, high-frequency tokens, yielding
degenerate output. Since the expert prefixes differ in length, each prefix is
therefore left-padded to a common length \(P_{\max}\), so that every canvas
takes the form \([\,\text{pad} \mid \text{prefix}_i \mid
\text{response}\,]\) with the response beginning at a common offset
\(P_{\max}\), and an attention mask excludes the padding from the computation.
This alignment is a precondition for the operator to behave as a product of
experts rather than as an average over misaligned contexts.

Importantly, this compositional operation is available to the masked-diffusion
model but not to an autoregressive one. An autoregressive model factorises the
joint distribution as \(p(\mathbf{r}) = \prod_j p(r_j \mid r_{<j}, \mathbf{c})\)
and commits each token before subsequent tokens are considered. It therefore
provides no shared, still-mutable response over which several
constraint-conditioned distributions can be multiplied position by position;
once \(r_{<j}\) is fixed, later constraints cannot revise it. The
Product-of-Experts construction requires simultaneous access to the entire
response as an editable object, a capability that masked-diffusion parallel
unmasking provides and autoregressive generation does not. From this
perspective, PoE is not merely an empirical variant of refinement but a
mechanism structurally tied to the diffusion process, and it is this
distinction that the capstone comparison in \Cref{sec:rq5} is designed to
examine.

\section{Backbone Abstraction and Encoder Capacity}
\label{sec:backbone}

The encoder that maps refined text into a conditioning representation determines
how much of the refined compositional structure is preserved during image
generation. As established in \Cref{ch:background}, this encoding introduces a
representational bottleneck in which explicit role-filler structure may become
entangled \citep{radford2021clip}. Rather than varying the encoder in
isolation, encoder capacity is here treated as a property of the T2I backbone,
and two backbones of differing conditioning capacity are compared:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Stable Diffusion 2.1}, which conditions on a single
        OpenCLIP-ViT-H text encoder at a resolution of \(768 \times 768\);
  \item \textbf{SDXL}, which conditions on two concatenated text encoders,
        OpenCLIP-ViT-bigG and CLIP-ViT-L, together with a pooled embedding, at
        a resolution of \(1024 \times 1024\).
\end{itemize}

Each backbone employs its native conditioning, so that the encoder is regarded
as a property of the backbone rather than as an independently varied component.
From this perspective, the comparison asks whether a higher-capacity
conditioning pathway alters the extent to which refinement improves
compositional generation. The refinement models and the evaluation protocol are
held fixed across backbones, and refined-text caches are shared between them, so
that only the image-side conditioning pathway differs. This backbone comparison
replaces the encoder-substitution axis considered in the original proposal,
while addressing the same underlying question of whether encoder capacity
constitutes the binding constraint on conditioning quality.

\section{Conditioning-Distortion Framework}
\label{sec:distortion}

The evaluation is grounded in the conditioning-distortion framework introduced
in the preceding chapters. The two conditioning branches are defined as:

\begin{equation}
c_{\mathrm{raw}} = f_{\mathrm{enc}}(t),
\qquad
c_{\mathrm{ref}} = f_{\mathrm{enc}}\!\left(g(t)\right)
\label{eq:conditioning_pair}
\end{equation}

where \(g\) denotes a refinement operator, instantiated in this study as the
autoregressive, masked-diffusion, or Product-of-Experts variant. Under
classifier-free guidance, the denoising direction for a given conditioning
signal \(c\) is:

\begin{equation}
\hat{\varepsilon}
= \varepsilon_\theta(x_t,\emptyset)
+ w\left[\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,c) - \varepsilon_\theta(x_t,\emptyset)\right]
\end{equation}

Decomposing the conditional noise prediction into an ideal component and a
conditioning-induced distortion term, \(\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,c) =
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ideal}}(x_t,c) + \delta(x_t,c)\), the central hypothesis of
the study is that refinement reduces the magnitude of this distortion term:

\begin{equation}
\left\| \delta\!\left(x_t,c_{\mathrm{ref}}\right) \right\|
<
\left\| \delta\!\left(x_t,c_{\mathrm{raw}}\right) \right\|
\label{eq:hypothesis}
\end{equation}

from which improved compositional fidelity and reduced sensitivity to the
guidance scale \(w\) are expected to follow. This framework provides the
theoretical basis for RQ1, which examines whether refinement produces more
structured conditioning, and RQ3, which examines whether refinement reduces
sensitivity to classifier-free guidance.

\section{Evaluation Framework}
\label{sec:eval}

Evaluation is structured around the research questions and their corresponding
hypotheses. To ensure that observed differences may be attributed to
conditioning alone, all configurations are generated under identical settings:
a fixed backbone without fine-tuning, identical sampling steps and noise
schedules, matched random seeds for paired comparisons, and controlled
classifier-free guidance scales.

\subsubsection{Semantic Alignment}

CLIPScore measures the semantic alignment between a generated image and a text
as the cosine similarity of their embeddings in CLIP space \citep{radford2021clip}:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{CLIPScore}(x, t)
= \cos\!\big(f_{\mathrm{img}}(x),\, f_{\mathrm{text}}(t)\big)
= \frac{f_{\mathrm{img}}(x) \cdot f_{\mathrm{text}}(t)}
       {\lVert f_{\mathrm{img}}(x)\rVert\,\lVert f_{\mathrm{text}}(t)\rVert}
\label{eq:clipscore}
\end{equation}

where:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \(x\) is a generated image and \(t\) is the text it is scored against,
  \item \(f_{\mathrm{img}}\) and \(f_{\mathrm{text}}\) are the CLIP image and
        text encoders respectively.
\end{itemize}

A methodological consideration specific to this study is the choice of scoring
target \(t\): every configuration is scored against the original prompt,
representing the user's intent, rather than against its own refined text.
Scoring an image against the very text that conditioned it would inflate the
refined configurations, since a longer and more detailed description is more
likely to match its own output, and would thereby invalidate the
cross-configuration comparison.

\subsubsection{Compositional Correctness}

Attribute binding accuracy measures whether attributes are assigned to the
correct objects, for instance whether \emph{red} is associated with \emph{cat}
and \emph{blue} with \emph{dog} in the prompt ``a red cat beside a blue dog''.
Relation accuracy correspondingly measures whether spatial relations such as
\emph{left of}, \emph{above}, or \emph{beside} are preserved. Both metrics are
computed as the mean over only those prompts to which the corresponding property
applies:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{acc}
= \frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}
  \sum_{i \in \mathcal{A}} \mathbb{1}\!\left[\text{prompt } i \text{ satisfied}\right]
\label{eq:accuracy}
\end{equation}

where:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \(\mathcal{A}\) is the set of prompts for which the property is
        applicable (those containing at least one attribute binding, or at
        least one relation, respectively),
  \item \(\mathbb{1}[\cdot]\) is the indicator function, equal to one when the
        binding or relation is correctly realised in the generated image and
        zero otherwise.
\end{itemize}

Restricting the average to \(\mathcal{A}\) is essential: prompts with no
applicable binding or relation are excluded rather than counted as failures,
since including them would introduce structural zeros that systematically
depress the reported accuracy independently of model performance.

\subsubsection{Guidance Robustness}

Compositional stability measures the robustness of compositional performance to
the classifier-free guidance scale \(w \in \{1, 3, 5, 7, 10\}\). It is defined as
one minus the mean coefficient of variation of the metrics across guidance
scales:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{stability}
= 1 - \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M} \mathrm{CV}_m,
\qquad
\mathrm{CV}_m = \frac{\sigma_m}{\lvert \mu_m \rvert}
\label{eq:stability}
\end{equation}

where:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \(M\) is the number of metrics aggregated (CLIPScore, attribute
        accuracy, relation accuracy),
  \item \(\mu_m\) and \(\sigma_m\) are the mean and standard deviation of
        metric \(m\) across the guidance scales.
\end{itemize}

The coefficient of variation, being normalised by the mean, is used in place of
raw variance so that metrics defined on different numerical scales remain
comparable. Lower variation across guidance scales, and hence higher stability,
is interpreted as evidence of reduced amplification of conditioning-induced
distortion.

\subsubsection{Conditioning Structure}

To quantify the geometry of the conditioning representation (RQ1), the mean
pairwise separation of a set of prompt embeddings is defined as their average
cosine distance:

\begin{equation}
\bar{d}
= \frac{2}{n(n-1)} \sum_{i < j}
  \Big(1 - \cos\!\big(e_i, e_j\big)\Big)
\label{eq:separation}
\end{equation}

where \(e_i\) is the encoded embedding of prompt \(i\) and \(n\) is the number
of prompts. The \emph{separation gain} of a refinement operator is then the
change in this quantity relative to the raw prompts,

\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\mathrm{sep}} = \bar{d}_{\mathrm{ref}} - \bar{d}_{\mathrm{raw}},
\end{equation}

so that a negative separation gain indicates that refinement compresses the
conditioning embeddings into a more similar region of the embedding space, and a
positive gain that it spreads them further apart. This measure is used in RQ1 as
an indirect indicator of whether the added compositional content survives
encoding or is discarded through entanglement.

\subsubsection{Perceptual Realism}

The Fr\'echet Inception Distance (FID) measures the distributional distance
between generated and reference images using feature statistics extracted from
an Inception network:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{FID}
= \lVert \mu_r - \mu_g \rVert_2^2
+ \mathrm{Tr}\!\left(
  \Sigma_r + \Sigma_g - 2\big(\Sigma_r \Sigma_g\big)^{1/2}
\right)
\label{eq:fid}
\end{equation}

where \((\mu_r, \Sigma_r)\) and \((\mu_g, \Sigma_g)\) denote the mean and
covariance of the real and generated feature distributions respectively. In this
study FID is reported as a secondary metric only. At the sample sizes used here
it is high-variance and biased upward, and it measures perceptual realism rather
than compositional correctness; it is therefore interpreted through relative
cross-configuration differences rather than through absolute magnitudes.

\subsubsection{Scene-Graph Extraction}

The structural measures used in RQ1 and the constraint decomposition used by
the Product-of-Experts operator are both obtained from a scene-graph extractor.
This component parses a text into objects, attribute bindings of the form
\([\text{object},\text{attribute}]\), and relation triples of the form
\([\text{object}_1,\text{relation},\text{object}_2]\), using an
instruction-tuned language model with a keyword-based fallback when the model
is unavailable. Employing the same extractor for both purposes ties the
analysis of conditioning structure and the decomposition used for composition
to a single underlying representation.

\section{RQ1: Conditioning Structure and Semantic Quality}
\label{sec:rq1}

The first research question examines whether diffusion-based refinement produces
more semantically structured conditioning signals than raw prompts, and whether
the encoder is able to preserve that structure. Following the conditioning-
distortion framework of \Cref{sec:distortion}, structure is measured in two
ways: through the compositional content of the refined text, quantified as
attribute and relation counts, and through the geometry of the resulting
conditioning embeddings, quantified as the mean pairwise cosine separation
between prompt embeddings. The former indicates whether refinement adds
compositional information; the latter indicates whether that information
survives encoding, or is compressed into a common region of the embedding
space.

\subsection{Refinement Adds Compositional Content}

Both refinement mechanisms substantially increase the compositional content of
the conditioning text relative to the raw prompt. Averaged across the prompt
set, the raw prompts contain \(1.24\) attribute tokens and \(0.37\) relation
tokens. Autoregressive refinement raises these to \(3.57\) and \(2.63\)
respectively, and masked-diffusion refinement to \(2.96\) and \(1.10\). In
absolute terms the autoregressive model adds more compositional tokens than the
masked-diffusion model, consistent with its tendency to produce longer and more
elaborately descriptive expansions. From this perspective, both mechanisms
succeed in the narrow sense of enriching the textual conditioning signal, and
the autoregressive model does so most aggressively.

\subsection{The Encoder Preserves This Structure Only at Sufficient Capacity}

The more consequential finding concerns whether this added structure survives
encoding. The separation gain, defined as the change in mean pairwise embedding
distance between raw and refined prompts, measures whether refinement causes the
conditioning embeddings to become more distinct or more clustered. A negative
separation gain indicates that refined prompts, despite carrying more
compositional content, are compressed into a more similar region of the
embedding space than the raw prompts from which they derive.

Under Stable Diffusion 2.1, refinement produces a pronounced negative
separation gain, most severely for autoregressive refinement. Under SDXL, whose
higher-capacity dual text encoders provide a substantially larger conditioning
representation, the same refinement produces almost no separation collapse.
\Cref{tab:rq1_separation} reports the comparison. On the single-encoder backbone
the autoregressive expansions, which add the most content, suffer the greatest
compression, reducing embedding separation by \(0.346\); the masked-diffusion
expansions degrade considerably less, by \(0.104\). On the dual-encoder backbone
both effects are close to zero, at \(-0.044\) and \(-0.006\) respectively.

\label{tab:rq1_separation}
\end{table}

This result may be interpreted as direct evidence for the representational
bottleneck argued in \Cref{ch:background}. Under a single-encoder backbone, the
compositional content added by refinement is not preserved by the encoder; the
additional attribute and relation tokens are compressed into a common region of
the embedding space, so that the conditioning signals of distinct refined
prompts become less, rather than more, distinguishable. The severity of this
effect scales with the amount of added content, which is why the autoregressive
mechanism, despite adding the most compositional tokens, suffers the greatest
collapse. From this perspective, the added structure is effectively discarded
at the encoding stage.

Under the higher-capacity backbone this compression is largely eliminated. The
dual-encoder conditioning pathway is able to represent the enriched prompts
without collapsing them toward one another, and the masked-diffusion mechanism
in particular preserves separation almost perfectly. This contrast provides the
empirical basis for the backbone comparison introduced in \Cref{sec:backbone}:
the conditioning bottleneck observed under Stable Diffusion 2.1 is not an
inherent property of prompt refinement, but a consequence of encoder capacity,
and it is substantially relieved once a more capable encoder is used. It was
this observation that motivated the adoption of SDXL as the primary backbone for
the remaining experiments.

\subsection{Summary}

RQ1 yields two findings. First, both refinement mechanisms enrich the
compositional content of the conditioning text, with the autoregressive
mechanism adding the most. Second, whether this enrichment survives encoding
depends critically on encoder capacity: under a single-encoder backbone the
added structure is compressed away, whereas under a dual-encoder backbone it is
largely preserved. The hypothesis that refinement produces more structured
conditioning is therefore supported only in conjunction with sufficient encoder
capacity, a qualification that frames the interpretation of the downstream
generation results in the sections that follow.

\paragraph{Note on measurement.} The attribute and relation counts reported in
this section were obtained using the keyword-based fallback of the scene-graph
extractor rather than its language-model backend, which was unavailable during
this run. The counts therefore represent a conservative lower bound on the
compositional content of each prompt, since the keyword lists do not capture
synonyms or less common attribute and relation terms. As the same extractor is
applied identically across all pipelines, the relative comparison between
mechanisms is unaffected; only the absolute magnitudes are conservative.

\section{RQ2: Compositional Benchmark}
\label{sec:rq2}

The second research question examines whether the enriched conditioning
signals produced by refinement reduce compositional errors in the generated
images, specifically attribute misbinding and relational inconsistency.
Evaluation is performed on the primary compositional benchmark under the SDXL
backbone, using attribute binding accuracy, relation accuracy, CLIPScore, and
FID. Following \Cref{sec:eval}, accuracy metrics are averaged only over prompts
to which the corresponding property applies, and the analysis focuses on the
three attribute sets with sufficient sample size, namely colour \((n = 240)\),
shape \((n = 50)\), and texture \((n = 50)\), together with the spatial
relation set \((n = 102)\).

\subsection{Attribute Binding}

\Cref{tab:rq2_binding} reports attribute binding accuracy for the three
well-sampled attribute sets. The most notable result is that raw prompting
achieves the highest binding accuracy on every set, exceeding both refinement
mechanisms. This finding is consistent across colour, shape, and texture, and
indicates that expansion of the conditioning text does not improve, and may
mildly degrade, the correctness of attribute-object associations in the
generated images.

\toprule

\midrule

\bottomrule

\label{tab:rq2_binding}
\end{table}

A second pattern emerges when the two refinement mechanisms are compared to one
another. Across all three attribute sets, masked-diffusion refinement achieves
higher binding accuracy than autoregressive refinement, by margins of between
one and three percentage points. Although neither mechanism surpasses raw
prompting, the masked-diffusion mechanism consistently produces the smaller
degradation. This ordering is consistent with the RQ1 finding that the
masked-diffusion mechanism preserves embedding separation more faithfully than
the autoregressive mechanism; the refinement that better preserves conditioning
structure also better preserves attribute binding downstream.

\subsection{Relational and Semantic Alignment}

On the spatial relation set, the only relation set with adequate sample size,
relation accuracy is \(0.422\) for raw prompting, \(0.412\) for masked-diffusion
refinement, and \(0.373\) for autoregressive refinement. Here the
masked-diffusion mechanism is approximately level with raw prompting, while the
autoregressive mechanism again lags. CLIPScore, which measures semantic
alignment against the original prompt, is highest for raw prompting across all
sets, mirroring the attribute binding result. From this perspective, the
semantic and compositional evaluations agree: expansion of the conditioning
text does not improve alignment with the intended scene, and among the two
mechanisms the masked-diffusion variant is consistently the stronger.

\subsection{Perceptual Realism}

FID values differ only marginally across the three configurations and do not
favour any one consistently, with raw prompting neither systematically better
nor worse than the refined variants. Consistent with the treatment of FID as a
secondary metric (\Cref{sec:eval}), no strong conclusion is drawn from these
values; they indicate only that refinement does not materially alter the
perceptual realism of the generated images, whatever its effect on
compositional correctness.

\subsection{Interpretation}

The finding that raw prompting achieves the highest binding accuracy is not an
artifact of the scoring procedure. Binding accuracy is evaluated against the
attribute-object pairs of the \emph{original} prompt (\Cref{eq:accuracy}), so
all three configurations are scored on an identical set of target bindings
regardless of how many additional attributes refinement introduces into the
conditioning text. The number of pairs checked, and the questions asked of the
vision-language model, are therefore fixed by the original prompt and do not
vary across configurations. Raw prompting does not win because it is scored on a
smaller or easier set of bindings.

Rather, the result reflects a genuine conditioning effect. Refinement injects
additional attributes and, in some cases, additional objects into the
conditioning text. While these additions do not alter the scoring targets, they
produce busier scenes in which the original target bindings must compete with
the added content for faithful rendering. The extra specification therefore
tends to corrupt, rather than reinforce, the very bindings the metric
evaluates. From this perspective, the minimal raw prompt renders its single
target binding reliably, whereas the enriched refined prompt renders the same
target binding less reliably because the model must simultaneously satisfy the
additional content that refinement has introduced.

This interpretation is consistent with the RQ1 analysis. There, the additional
compositional content introduced by refinement was shown to be poorly preserved
by the encoder under the single-encoder backbone, and only partially preserved
under the dual-encoder backbone. Content that is entangled at the conditioning
stage manifests downstream as attribute bleed in the generated image, in which
an attribute intended for one object is rendered on another. The RQ2 result may
therefore be read as the image-space consequence of the conditioning-space
effect measured in RQ1: refinement adds compositional content, that content is
imperfectly represented in the conditioning signal, and its imperfect
representation degrades the original bindings during generation.

\subsection{Summary}

The RQ2 results do not support the hypothesis that prompt refinement reduces
compositional errors. Under the SDXL backbone, raw prompting achieves the
highest attribute binding accuracy and the highest semantic alignment on every
set, and refinement produces at best a neutral and at worst a mildly negative
effect. As argued above, this is a genuine conditioning effect rather than a
scoring artifact: refinement adds content that, being imperfectly preserved
through encoding, competes with and degrades the original target bindings. The
one clear internal distinction is that masked-diffusion refinement consistently
outperforms autoregressive refinement, foreshadowing the mechanism comparison
examined directly in RQ4. Notably, the absolute accuracies under SDXL are
substantially higher than those observed under Stable Diffusion 2.1 across all
configurations, which is consistent with the RQ1 finding that the
higher-capacity backbone provides a more faithful conditioning pathway.

\section{RQ3: Classifier-Free Guidance Sensitivity}
\label{sec:rq3}

The third research question examines whether refinement reduces sensitivity to
the classifier-free guidance scale \(w\). For each configuration, images are
generated across the guidance scales \(w \in \{1, 3, 5, 7, 10\}\) using matched
seeds, and compositional stability is computed as one minus the mean coefficient
of variation of the metrics across scales (\Cref{sec:eval}). Higher stability
indicates less sensitivity to the choice of guidance scale.

\Cref{tab:rq3_stability} reports compositional stability for the three
configurations. Raw prompting and autoregressive refinement are almost
indistinguishable, at \(0.900\) and \(0.899\) respectively, while
masked-diffusion refinement is somewhat lower, at \(0.871\). Contrary to the
hypothesis that refinement reduces guidance sensitivity, refinement does not
improve stability; the masked-diffusion mechanism in particular exhibits
slightly greater sensitivity to the guidance scale than raw prompting.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lc}
\toprule
\textbf{Configuration} & \textbf{Compositional stability} \\
\midrule
Raw                      & 0.900 \\
Autoregressive           & 0.899 \\
Masked-diffusion (LLaDA) & 0.871 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption[Compositional stability under SDXL]{Compositional stability across
guidance scales \(w \in \{1,3,5,7,10\}\) under the SDXL backbone. Higher values

\label{tab:rq3_stability}

This result should be interpreted with caution, since the relation component of
the compositional-stability measure is computed over a small sweep set in which
only a few prompts carry an applicable spatial relation. At this sample size the
relation-accuracy values across guidance scales are dominated by the outcome of
a small number of prompts, so the corresponding contribution to the stability
measure is subject to substantial sampling noise. The CLIPScore and attribute
components, which are computed over more prompts, provide a more reliable
signal, and on these components the three configurations behave similarly across
guidance scales. The overall finding is therefore best characterised as an
absence of evidence that refinement reduces guidance sensitivity, rather than as
evidence of a strong difference between configurations.

\section{RQ4: Mechanism Comparison}
\label{sec:rq4}

The fourth research question isolates the effect of the refinement mechanism,
asking whether the autoregressive and masked-diffusion mechanisms differ in
their downstream compositional performance when applied to the same prompts
under identical conditions. Because both mechanisms receive the same expansion
instruction and are evaluated under matched seeds and guidance settings, any
difference between them may be attributed to the underlying generative process.
The comparison is expressed as the difference in each metric between the
masked-diffusion and autoregressive configurations, with positive values
indicating that the masked-diffusion mechanism performs better.

\Cref{tab:rq4_deltas} reports these differences on the sets for which each
metric is applicable. The masked-diffusion mechanism outperforms the
autoregressive mechanism on the majority of the applicable comparisons. On
colour binding it improves attribute accuracy by \(0.015\); on the spatial set
it improves relation accuracy by \(0.039\); and on the cats-and-dogs set it
improves attribute accuracy by \(0.028\). The differences are modest in
magnitude but consistent in direction, with the masked-diffusion mechanism
favoured on four of the six well-defined comparisons.

higher-capacity backbone a consistent, if small, advantage for the
masked-diffusion mechanism emerges. From this perspective, the mechanism does
appear to matter once the encoder is capable of preserving the refined
conditioning structure, which is consistent with the RQ1 finding that the
masked-diffusion mechanism better preserves embedding separation. The single
negative value, on the cats-and-dogs relation set, is computed over only eight
applicable prompts and should not be interpreted as evidence against this
pattern.

Taken together with RQ2, the mechanism comparison supports a nuanced
conclusion. Neither refinement mechanism improves compositional generation
relative to raw prompting, yet among the mechanisms the masked-diffusion variant
is consistently the stronger. The advantage is real but small, and it is
confined to the regime of single-prompt expansion. This observation motivates a
further question, namely whether the masked-diffusion mechanism possesses a
compositional capability that the autoregressive mechanism structurally lacks,
and which single-prompt expansion does not exercise.

\section{RQ6: Tunability}
\label{sec:rq6}

The final research question examines how the two layers of the pipeline respond
to their principal hyperparameters, and in particular how quality and
computational cost trade off across them. Two knobs are considered on each
layer. On the language layer, the number of masked-diffusion unmasking steps and
the generation length govern how the refined prompt is produced; on the image
layer, the number of denoising steps and the classifier-free guidance scale
govern how the image is generated. These are swept jointly over a grid, and
each configuration is evaluated by its mean CLIPScore and its wall-clock
generation time. Because the sweep is intended to characterise the shape of the
cost and quality surfaces rather than to benchmark, it is conducted over a small
fixed prompt subset, and the reported values should be read as coarse trends
across the grid rather than as precise per-configuration estimates.

\subsection{Guidance is the Effective Quality Parameter}

Of the parameters considered, the classifier-free guidance scale is the only one
that materially affects semantic alignment. \Cref{fig:rq6_saturation} plots mean
CLIPScore against the guidance scale, with a separate line for each image-step
count. Two features are apparent. First, CLIPScore increases with the guidance
scale, but with strongly diminishing returns: the largest gain occurs between
the lowest guidance values, from \(w = 2\) to \(w = 5\), after which the metric
plateaus. The relationship is therefore concave and saturating rather than
linear, with the majority of the achievable improvement realised by a moderate
guidance scale of approximately \(w = 5\). Second, the lines for the different
image-step counts lie almost on top of one another, indicating that the number
of denoising steps has little effect on CLIPScore. Increasing the step count
from ten to one hundred leaves the metric essentially unchanged.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
% \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{images/rq6_cfg_saturation}
\caption[CLIPScore versus guidance scale]{Mean CLIPScore as a function of the
classifier-free guidance scale, with one line per image-step count and each
point averaged over the language-layer configurations of the grid. CLIPScore
rises steeply from \(w = 2\) to \(w = 5\) and then plateaus, and the step-count
lines nearly coincide, indicating that guidance is the effective quality
parameter while the step count is not.}
\label{fig:rq6_saturation}
\end{figure}

The same pattern holds on the language layer. Varying the number of unmasking
steps or the generation length leaves CLIPScore essentially unchanged once the
refined prompt is produced at all. From this perspective, the step counts on
both layers exhibit diminishing returns: beyond a minimum number of steps,
further steps do not improve measured quality.

An important qualification concerns the location of this minimum. Within the
sampled range, from ten to one hundred image steps and from thirty-two to one
hundred and twenty-eight unmasking steps, quality is already flat at the lowest
sampled values. This indicates that the minimum number of steps required to
reach saturation lies at or below the smallest values tested, so that while the
plateau is clearly observed, the precise threshold is not identified by this
grid. Locating it would require sampling below the current minimum, which is
noted as a direction for future work.

\subsection{Cost Scales with Steps at Constant Quality}

While the step counts have little effect on quality, they are the dominant
determinant of computational cost. \Cref{fig:rq6_time} plots mean image
generation time against the number of denoising steps. Generation time grows
approximately linearly with the step count, from roughly two seconds at ten
steps to roughly twenty seconds at one hundred steps, and is largely independent
of the guidance scale. Combined with the language-layer rewrite time, which
scales with both unmasking steps and generation length, the total time per
prompt ranges over roughly a factor of seven across the grid, from
approximately four seconds in the cheapest configuration to approximately thirty
seconds in the most expensive.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
% \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{images/rq6_time}
\caption[Generation cost versus image steps]{Mean image generation time as a
function of the number of denoising steps, averaged over the remaining grid
dimensions. Cost grows approximately linearly with the step count and is
largely independent of the guidance scale.}
\label{fig:rq6_time}
\end{figure}

Taken together, these two observations yield a clear efficiency conclusion.
Across a sevenfold range in computational cost, CLIPScore varies by less than
the gain obtained from a single increment of the guidance scale. The most
expensive configurations therefore purchase almost no measurable improvement in
semantic alignment over the cheapest. A moderate guidance scale of
approximately \(w = 5\) combined with a low step count captures nearly all of
the achievable CLIPScore at a small fraction of the maximum cost.

\subsection{Perceptual and Semantic Effects of Guidance}

The preceding analysis concerns CLIPScore, which measures semantic alignment.
The visual effect of the guidance scale is more pronounced than this metric
suggests. \Cref{fig:rq6_grid} shows generated images across the two tuning axes
for a representative prompt. Along the guidance axis the images change markedly,
from washed-out and low-contrast at \(w = 2\) to saturated and high-contrast at
\(w = 12\), whereas along the step axis they change very little. This is
consistent with the CLIPScore analysis in identifying guidance as the effective
parameter, and it further indicates that the perceptual influence of guidance
extends beyond the point at which its effect on semantic alignment has
saturated: increasing the guidance scale beyond \(w = 5\) continues to alter the
appearance of the image, eventually toward oversaturation, without a
corresponding change in CLIPScore.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
% \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{images/rq6_image_grid}
\caption[Generated images across the tuning axes]{Generated images for a
representative prompt across the guidance scale (columns) and the language-layer
step count (rows). The guidance axis produces a pronounced perceptual change,
from low-contrast at low guidance to oversaturated at high guidance, while the
step axis produces little visible change, mirroring the CLIPScore analysis.}
\label{fig:rq6_grid}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Summary}

RQ6 characterises the tunability of the two-layer pipeline. Among the four
hyperparameters considered, the classifier-free guidance scale is the single
effective control over semantic quality, and even its effect saturates by a
moderate value of approximately \(w = 5\). The step counts on both layers
exhibit diminishing returns, with quality already flat at the lowest sampled
values, so that the minimum number of steps required is not resolved by this
grid. Computational cost, by contrast, scales approximately linearly with the
step count, producing a sevenfold range in cost across the grid at nearly
constant CLIPScore. The practical implication is that a moderate guidance scale
combined with a low step count is close to optimal in the cost-quality sense, a
finding relevant to any deployment in which inference cost is a consideration.
